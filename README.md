# Cement Finance Research

## Overview

This project is building an evidence based view of how cement plants are financed and under what conditions different forms of capital are most effective.

The central research question is:

> Under what conditions can third party, project level equity unlock cement and low carbon cement projects more effectively than corporate balance sheet capital, local bank debt, development finance institution debt, or blended finance?

The objective is not to prove that equity is always superior. The objective is to identify where equity solves a genuine financing constraint, where debt is more efficient, and where a blended capital structure is likely to produce the best outcome.

This matters because discussions about industrial decarbonization often focus on technology, emissions, and total capital requirements without examining the structure of the capital itself. A technically attractive cement project may still fail to proceed because the producer cannot allocate enough balance sheet capital, local debt is too expensive or too short term, construction and ramp up risks are difficult to finance, or no investor is willing to coordinate the project level capital structure.

## Why this research is important

Cement is a capital intensive, emissions intensive industry with thousands of production facilities operating across very different financial markets.

A large multinational producer financing a standardized retrofit in Europe faces a very different capital problem from a regional cement company developing a greenfield calcined clay plant in Africa or South Asia. In one case, corporate funding or low cost debt may be the obvious solution. In the other, third party equity may be required to absorb development risk, reduce debt service during ramp up, and preserve the producer’s balance sheet.

There is no widely available global database showing how individual cement plants were financed. Existing plant databases are strong on location, ownership, capacity, operating status, and technology, but generally do not describe the underlying capital structure.

This project therefore separates two questions:

1. What cement plants exist?
2. How were the relevant construction, expansion, retrofit, refinancing, acquisition, or decarbonization events financed?

The distinction is important. The unit of analysis for the investment thesis is not simply the physical cement plant. It is the financing event associated with that plant.

## What has been completed

### 1. Research methodology

A research framework has been created to distinguish among:

* Corporate balance sheet funding
* Sponsor cash equity
* Retained earnings
* Contributed assets
* Third party institutional equity
* Development finance institution equity
* Shareholder loans
* Corporate debt
* Project finance debt
* Local bank debt
* Development finance institution debt
* Export credit financing
* Grants
* Guarantees
* Political risk insurance
* Foreign exchange protection
* Blended and concessional capital

The methodology requires confirmed facts, estimates, and inferences to be recorded separately. It also requires financing amounts, project costs, commitments, disbursements, ownership rights, guarantees, and recourse arrangements to be treated as distinct concepts.

A transaction is not classified as equity unless the source of the capital and the associated ownership rights can be identified.

### 2. Initial transaction research

Five financing cases were researched to test the methodology:

* Senj Sant Cement, Mongolia
* CBI Ghana
* Dangote Cement Mtwara, Tanzania
* Cimpor Côte d’Ivoire
* UltraTech green capital expenditure and waste heat recovery investments, India

These cases produced several preliminary findings.

Senj Sant and CBI Ghana provide evidence of development finance institution equity combined with debt and sponsor capital.

Dangote Mtwara and Cimpor Côte d’Ivoire demonstrate that public information often describes plant cost, capacity, and operating outcomes without revealing the full capital structure.

UltraTech provides an important counterexample. A large, creditworthy incumbent may be better served by corporate funding for repeatable efficiency and waste heat recovery investments.

The initial evidence therefore supports a conditional rather than universal equity thesis.

### 3. Global plant universe

Two asset level cement datasets were imported and reviewed:

* Global Energy Monitor Global Cement and Concrete Tracker
* Spatial Finance Initiative Global Database of Cement Production Assets

The original source workbooks were preserved without modification and verified using file hashes.

The source data contained:

| Source | Global records | Records outside China |
|---|---:|---:|
| Global Energy Monitor | 3,515 | 2,421 |
| Spatial Finance Initiative | 3,117 | 1,958 |

The two sources differ in scope, field structure, ownership information, operating status, plant naming, and capacity definitions. They could not simply be appended together.

A reconciliation methodology was therefore developed using geography, ownership, corporate relationships, plant type, capacity, operating status, commissioning year, and available source identifiers.

The reconciliation produced:

| Result | Count |
|---|---:|
| Confirmed matches | 1,455 |
| Probable matches | 8 |
| Possible matches requiring review | 1,088 |
| Global Energy Monitor only records | 795 |
| Spatial Finance only records | 425 |
| Provisional unique plants outside China | 2,550 |
| Manual review cases | 1,130 |
| Ownership conflicts | 916 |
| Capacity conflicts above 10 percent | 917 |

The resulting provisional plant universe contains 2,550 cement production sites outside China.

Conflicting ownership, capacity, and location values were preserved rather than silently resolved. Only confirmed matches and qualifying high confidence unmatched records received permanent plant identifiers.

### 4. Financing event screening system

The 2,550 plant universe was screened to identify facilities most likely to have relevant financing events.

The screening system considers:

* Commissioning or major investment since 2000
* Greenfield construction
* Major expansion
* Retrofit or modernization
* Low carbon technology
* Acquisition or refinancing
* Restructuring or distress
* Emerging market location
* Likely project size
* Evidence availability
* Relevance to development finance
* Relevance to third party equity
* Relevance to industrial decarbonization

The screening produced:

| Research tier | Plants |
|---|---:|
| Tier 1 | 877 |
| Tier 2 | 654 |
| Tier 3 | 661 |
| Tier 4 | 358 |

A proposed stratified sample of 100 plants has also been created for detailed financing research.

The sample is designed to include positive and negative cases across regions, project types, sponsor types, operating outcomes, and potential financing structures.

The current screening classifications are research hypotheses, not confirmed financing conclusions. Development finance institution involvement, third party equity, local bank debt, and corporate funding remain unknown until transaction level evidence is collected.

## How the work has been conducted

The project follows several operating principles.

### Preserve the original evidence

Original datasets and source documents are retained unchanged. Standardized, cleaned, and reconciled files are created separately.

### Maintain an audit trail

Every major transformation, field mapping, match rule, exclusion, and methodological decision is documented.

### Avoid false precision

Records are not merged solely because they share similar names, owners, or coordinates. Unresolved conflicts remain visible.

### Separate plants from financing events

A physical plant may have several capital events during its life, including initial construction, expansion, refinancing, acquisition, retrofit, and restructuring. These events will be recorded separately and linked to the permanent plant identifier.

### Use primary sources first

Detailed transaction research prioritizes:

1. Company annual reports and audited financial statements
2. Stock exchange filings
3. Development finance institution disclosures
4. Lender documents
5. Government filings
6. Bond prospectuses
7. Credit rating reports
8. Court and restructuring records

Press releases, trade publications, and news reports are used as supporting evidence where primary documents are unavailable.

### Include evidence against the thesis

The sample is not designed to select only visible development finance or equity transactions.

Corporate funded projects, local bank financed plants, failed projects, restructurings, and cases with no evidence of third party equity are necessary to test the thesis objectively.

## Preliminary investment thesis

The evidence collected so far suggests the following working thesis:

> Third party, project level equity may be most valuable for commercially attractive cement projects where the producer has operating capability and market access but faces limited balance sheet appetite, expensive or short term debt, uncertain construction and ramp up risk, or first of a kind technology and market adoption risk.

The early evidence also suggests important limitations:

* Equity is unlikely to be the most efficient capital for every project.
* Corporate funding may be superior for standardized retrofits undertaken by large and creditworthy producers.
* Development finance institution debt may be more efficient when project cash flows are predictable and debt service can be supported.
* Blended equity, guarantees, and long tenor debt may offer better risk adjusted outcomes than either pure equity or highly leveraged local debt.
* Public disclosure is substantially better for development finance institution transactions than for many corporate financed projects, creating a potential evidence bias.

These are preliminary findings and should not yet be treated as final conclusions.

## What comes next

### 1. Validate and freeze the 100 plant sample

The proposed research sample will be reviewed for:

* Regional balance
* Project type balance
* Concentration among large multinational producers
* Representation of regional and family owned producers
* Successful and distressed projects
* Development finance institution and non development finance institution cases
* Corporate funded and project financed cases
* Low carbon and conventional investments

The screening methodology and sample selection rules will then be frozen as version 1.0.

### 2. Confirm financing events

For each selected plant, the research will first determine whether a qualifying financing event occurred after 2000.

Events may include:

* Greenfield construction
* Capacity expansion
* Grinding or clinker investment
* Calcined clay or LC3 investment
* Major retrofit
* Decarbonization investment
* Acquisition
* Refinancing
* Restructuring
* Closure or failed development

Detailed capital structure research will begin only after the event itself is confirmed.

### 3. Reconstruct capital structures

For each qualifying event, the research will seek to identify:

* Total project cost
* Sponsor cash equity
* Contributed assets
* Third party equity
* Development finance institution equity
* Shareholder loans
* Corporate debt
* Project debt
* Local bank debt
* Development finance institution debt
* Grants and guarantees
* Interest rate
* Tenor
* Currency
* Recourse
* Sponsor support
* Ownership rights
* Operating outcome

Unavailable information will remain unknown rather than being estimated without evidence.

### 4. Compare financing models

The completed evidence will be used to compare representative cement projects under several capital structures:

* Fully corporate funded
* Sponsor equity plus local debt
* Sponsor equity plus development finance institution debt
* Third party project level equity
* Blended equity, debt, and guarantees

The analysis will examine:

* Producer capital required
* Project resilience
* Debt service coverage
* Cash requirements during ramp up
* Producer return on invested capital
* Capacity created per dollar of producer capital
* Emissions reduced per dollar of producer capital
* Exposure to construction delay
* Exposure to cost overruns
* Exposure to currency depreciation
* Exposure to lower sales volume and pricing

### 5. Produce final outputs

Planned outputs include:

* A global cement plant and financing event database
* A full evidence report
* A ten page investment thesis memo
* A two page investment committee summary
* A financial comparison model
* A public methodology note
* A potential article titled “What the Financing of Cement Plants Can Teach Us About Industrial Decarbonization”

## Value added by this project

The principal value of this work is not the creation of another cement plant directory.

The project connects four bodies of information that are usually separate:

1. Physical cement assets
2. Ownership and corporate structure
3. Capital investment events
4. Financing structures and operating outcomes

This creates a basis for answering questions that existing plant databases cannot answer:

* Which cement projects received third party equity?
* Which relied on sponsor balance sheets?
* When did local debt create a meaningful constraint?
* When did development finance institutions provide equity rather than only debt?
* Did equity enable projects that would otherwise not have proceeded?
* Did third party capital preserve producer balance sheets?
* Which structures performed better during construction and ramp up?
* Which financing models are best suited to low carbon cement projects in emerging markets?

The research can therefore provide value to several audiences.

### Investors

It can help investors identify where project level equity provides genuine additionality and where it merely replaces capital that a producer could have supplied itself.

### Cement producers

It can help producers compare the capital efficiency of funding projects directly against partnering with institutional investors.

### Development finance institutions

It can help development institutions determine where debt, equity, guarantees, or blended structures are most catalytic.

### Climate funders

It can help philanthropic and public funders identify the financing gaps that prevent technically credible industrial decarbonization projects from reaching construction.

### Project developers

It can provide a framework for designing projects that are technically credible, commercially viable, and financeable.
